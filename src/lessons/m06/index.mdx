## What is this about?

The notes open a new chapter: <Term en="Time-domain characteristics of control systems" tr="Kontrol Sistemlerinin Zaman Bölgesi Karakteristikleri" /> <Src p="1" />. Until now we built transfer functions. Now we ask the question an engineer actually cares about: **if I apply this input, what will the output do over time, and how fast, and how accurately?**

We start with the simplest dynamic system there is: the <Term en="first-order control system" tr="Birinci Dereceden Kontrol Sistemi" />.

**A concrete picture first.** Close a switch that connects a battery to a resistor and an uncharged capacitor. The capacitor voltage does not jump to the battery voltage; it rises quickly at first and then more and more slowly, creeping up to the final value. Put a cup of hot tea in a room: its temperature falls fast at first, then slowly. Press the accelerator of a car: speed rises and levels off. All of these have the same shape, because each one obeys the same rule:

> **The rate of change is proportional to how far you are from where you are going.**

Far from the target → change fast. Close to the target → change slowly. That rule is a first-order differential equation, and its fingerprint is the exponential $e^{-t/\tau}$. A single number, the **time constant** $\tau$, tells you how fast everything happens.

This module answers, for a first-order system:

- What does the output do for the three standard test inputs (impulse, step, ramp — defined in [Module 02](#/m/02))?
- What is the meaning of $\tau$ on each graph, and how do you sketch each graph by hand?
- How long until the output has "settled"?
- How big is the error that remains forever (the steady-state error $e_{ss}$)?
- What changes when the gain is not 1 (Example 1) or when the transfer function has an $s$ in the numerator (Example 2)?

**After this module you can:**

- reduce any unity-feedback loop with a first-order $G(s)$ to the form $T(s)=\dfrac{K}{\tau s+1}$ and read off $\tau$, the DC gain $K$, and the pole $-1/\tau$;
- write the impulse, step and ramp responses immediately, and sketch them with the correct tangents, key points and final values;
- compute settling time ($4\tau$–$5\tau$) and the steady-state error both in the time domain and with $\lim_{s\to0}s[R(s)-Y(s)]$;
- use the initial and final value theorems — and recognise when they need care;
- read $\tau$ and $K$ from a measured response curve.

The Laplace tools used here (partial fractions, inverse transforms, the initial and final value theorems) come from [Module 03](#/m/03).

## The standard first-order loop

### The block diagram

The notes start from this loop <Src p="1" />:

<FirstOrderLoopBD />

- $R(s)$ — the input (reference, the value we want).
- $E(s)$ — the error, $E(s)=R(s)-Y(s)$: what comes out of the summing junction $\Sigma$ with signs $+$ (from $R$) and $-$ (from $Y$).
- $\dfrac{1}{\tau s}$ — the forward-path transfer function $G(s)$. $\tau$ is a positive constant measured in seconds.
- $Y(s)$ — the output. It is fed straight back (no block in the feedback path, so $H(s)=1$): this is <Term en="unity negative feedback" tr="birim negatif geri besleme" />.

### Deriving T(s)

<Example title="Deriving T(s) for the standard first-order loop" src="1">

<Step title="Write the loop equations">

From the diagram: the error is $E(s)=R(s)-Y(s)$, and the block gives $Y(s)=G(s)E(s)$ with $G(s)=\dfrac{1}{\tau s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Eliminate E(s)">

$$
Y(s)=G(s)\big[R(s)-Y(s)\big]\;\Rightarrow\;Y(s)\big[1+G(s)\big]=G(s)R(s)\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{G(s)}{1+G(s)}
$$

This is the familiar closed-loop formula for unity negative feedback.

</Step>

<Step title="Substitute G(s) and clear the fraction">

$$
T(s)=\frac{\dfrac{1}{\tau s}}{1+\dfrac{1}{\tau s}}
$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $\tau s$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{1}{\tau s+1}
$$

</Step>

<Answer>

$$
T(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{1}{\tau s+1}
$$

</Answer>

</Example>

<KeyFormula title="Standard first-order closed-loop transfer function">

$$
T(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{1}{\tau s+1}
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Src p="1" /> The denominator is first-order in $s$ (highest power $s^1$) — that is exactly what "first-order system" means. The notes then say: *"Ters Laplace dönüşümünü alırsak sistemin birim impulse tepkesini buluruz"* — "if we take the inverse Laplace transform we find the system's unit impulse response."

### The differential equation behind it

Why does this loop behave like the capacitor or the cooling tea? Read the block $\dfrac{1}{\tau s}$ in the time domain: $\dfrac{1}{s}$ is integration, so the block integrates the error and multiplies it by $\dfrac{1}{\tau}$. Equivalently, the derivative of the output is proportional to the error:

<IntegratorViewBD />

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{\tau s}E(s)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\tau s\,Y(s)=E(s)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\tau\frac{dy}{dt}=e(t)=r(t)-y(t)
$$

(using $\L\{\dot y\}=sY(s)$ with zero initial conditions). Rearranged:

$$
\tau\frac{dy}{dt}+y(t)=r(t)
$$

and its Laplace transform is $(\tau s+1)Y(s)=R(s)$, i.e. $T(s)=\dfrac{1}{\tau s+1}$ again.

<Intuition>

$\dfrac{dy}{dt}=\dfrac{e(t)}{\tau}$ is the whole story of this module:

- **Big error → output moves fast.** At the start of a step, $e=1$, so the slope is $1/\tau$.
- **Error shrinks → output slows down.** That is why the curve bends over exponentially.
- **The output keeps moving as long as there is any error at all.** So for a constant target it can only stop when $e=0$ → zero steady-state error for a step.
- **To follow a ramp of slope 1**, the output needs $\dfrac{dy}{dt}=1$, which requires a constant error $e=\tau$. That is the constant lag $\tau$ we will find for the ramp.
- **Smaller $\tau$** → the same error produces a bigger slope → a faster system.

</Intuition>

### Physical twin: the RC circuit

The RC circuit (see [Module 05](#/m/05)) is the textbook physical first-order system:

<RCCircuit />

- KVL around the loop: $v_s(t)=R\,i(t)+v_c(t)$.
- Capacitor law: $i(t)=C\dfrac{dv_c}{dt}$.
- Substituting: $RC\dfrac{dv_c}{dt}+v_c(t)=v_s(t)$.

This is **exactly** $\tau\dot y+y=r$ with $y=v_c$, $r=v_s$ and $\tau=RC$. In the $s$-domain:

$$
\frac{V_c(s)}{V_s(s)}=\frac{1}{RCs+1}
$$

The "error" is the voltage across the resistor, $v_s-v_c$; it drives the current that charges the capacitor. When the capacitor reaches $v_s$, the resistor voltage — the error — is zero and charging stops. Everything said below about $\tau$ applies to this circuit with $\tau=RC$.

<Example title="RC circuit as a first-order system">

**Given:** $R=1\,\text{k}\Omega$, $C=100\,\mu\text{F}$, a 5 V step is applied at $t=0$, capacitor initially uncharged. **Asked:** $\tau$, $v_c(t)$, the voltage after one time constant, and the settling time.

<Step title="Time constant">

$\tau=RC=(1000\,\Omega)(100\times10^{-6}\,\text{F})=0.1\,\text{s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Response">

The input is $5\cdot u(t)$, so $V_s(s)=\dfrac{5}{s}$ and, by linearity, the output is 5 times the unit step response (derived below):

$$
v_c(t)=5\left(1-e^{-t/0.1}\right)=5\left(1-e^{-10t}\right)\ \text{V}
$$

</Step>

<Step title="Key values">

At $t=\tau=0.1$ s: $v_c=5(1-e^{-1})=5(0.632)=3.16$ V. Settling: $4\tau=0.4$ s (98.2 %), $5\tau=0.5$ s (99.3 %).

</Step>

<Answer>

$\tau=0.1$ s, $v_c(t)=5(1-e^{-10t})$ V, $v_c(0.1)=3.16$ V, settled after about 0.4–0.5 s.

</Answer>

**Sanity check:** $v_c(0)=0$ (the capacitor starts uncharged), $v_c(\infty)=5$ V (a fully charged capacitor carries no current, so it has the whole source voltage). ✓

</Example>

### Where is the pole?

The denominator $\tau s+1$ is zero at

$$
s=-\frac{1}{\tau}
$$

This single real pole lies on the negative real axis. Every response of the system contains the term $e^{-t/\tau}=e^{(-1/\tau)t}$, i.e. $e^{pt}$ with $p$ the pole. Consequently:

- **pole farther to the left** ⇒ $1/\tau$ larger ⇒ $\tau$ smaller ⇒ **faster** decay;
- **pole close to the origin** ⇒ $\tau$ large ⇒ slow, sluggish system;
- pole on the left half-plane ⇒ $e^{-t/\tau}\to0$ ⇒ the system is stable and the transient dies out.

You can move the pole yourself in the explorer further below.

## Unit impulse response

<Def term="Unit impulse response" tr="Birim Impulse Tepkesi">

The output when the input is the unit impulse $r(t)=\delta(t)$, whose Laplace transform is $R(s)=1$ (see [Module 02](#/m/02)). Since $Y(s)=T(s)\cdot1$, the impulse response is simply the inverse Laplace transform of the transfer function. <Src p="1" />

</Def>

### Derivation

Write $T(s)$ in the form of a standard transform pair. Use $\Li\left\{\dfrac{1}{s+a}\right\}=e^{-at}$ after dividing numerator and denominator by $\tau$:

$$
\frac{1}{\tau s+1}=\frac{1}{\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{s+\frac{1}{\tau}}
$$

so

$$
y(t)=\Li\left\{1\cdot\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\right\}=\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}},\qquad t\ge0
$$

<KeyFormula title="Unit impulse response of 1/(τs+1)">

$$
y(t)=\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}
$$

</KeyFormula>

### The graph and the meaning of tau

<Src p="1" /> The notes sketch this decaying exponential, starting at $\frac{1}{\tau}$, with a dashed tangent from the starting point down to the time axis at $t=\tau$ and ticks at $\tau$, $4\tau$, $5\tau$:

<ImpulsePlot />

- **Axes:** time $t$ horizontally, output $y(t)$ vertically. The plot uses $\tau=1$ s so that the time axis directly reads in multiples of $\tau$.
- **Start:** $y(0^+)=\frac{1}{\tau}$. The impulse "kicks" the system: it instantly delivers a finite amount of output, then the system relaxes.
- **After one time constant:** $y(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-1}=0.368\cdot\frac{1}{\tau}$ — the response has dropped to 36.8 % of its initial value (it has lost 63.2 %).
- **Final value:** $y\to0$.
- **Shape:** concave up, always positive, never actually reaching zero.

The professor's comment: *"Görüldüğü gibi τ zaman sabitini temsil etmektedir. Sistemin sönüm süresi yaklaşık 4τ–5τ olarak alınabilir."* — "As seen, $\tau$ represents the <Term en="time constant" tr="zaman sabiti" />. The system's <Term en="decay time" tr="sönüm süresi" /> can be taken as approximately $4\tau$–$5\tau$." <Src p="1" />

How much is left after $n$ time constants? $e^{-n}$ of the initial value:

| $t$ | $\tau$ | $2\tau$ | $3\tau$ | $4\tau$ | $5\tau$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $e^{-t/\tau}$ (fraction remaining) | 0.368 | 0.135 | 0.050 | 0.018 | 0.007 |

After $4\tau$ only 1.8 % remains, after $5\tau$ only 0.7 % — practically zero. That is why $4\tau$–$5\tau$ is the "decay time".

### Initial slope of the impulse response

Differentiate (chain rule: $\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t/\tau}=-\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}$):

$$
\left.\frac{dy(t)}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\left.-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right|_{t=0}=-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}
$$

<Src p="2" /> *"Burada t=0 anında y(t) fonksiyonunun eğimi $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$ olarak bulunur"* — "here the <Term en="slope" tr="eğim" /> of $y(t)$ at $t=0$ is $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$."

**Why the tangent hits the axis exactly at $\tau$:** the tangent line starts at height $\frac{1}{\tau}$ and falls with slope $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$, so the line is $\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{t}{\tau^2}$. It is zero when $t=\tau$. In words: *if the response kept falling at its initial rate, it would be gone after exactly one time constant.* This gives a graphical way to find $\tau$ from a measured curve.

<Note title="Correction to the notes (p. 2)">

The notes write the middle expression as $\left.\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}e^{-t/\tau}\right|_{t=0}$ — without the minus sign — and then the final result as $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$. The derivative of $\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}$ is $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}e^{-t/\tau}$ (the inner derivative of $-t/\tau$ is $-\frac{1}{\tau}$). The **final result $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$ is correct**; only the minus sign was dropped in the intermediate step. A decaying curve must have a negative slope.

</Note>

### Steady-state error definition

<Def term="Steady-state error" tr="Kalıcı durum hatası">

The error that remains after the transient has died out:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{t\to\infty}e(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\big[r(t)-y(t)\big]
$$

or, with the final value theorem applied to $E(s)=R(s)-Y(s)$:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\big[R(s)-Y(s)\big]
$$

<Src p="2" />

</Def>

The $s$-domain version comes from the final value theorem $\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)=\lim_{s\to0}sF(s)$ ([Module 03](#/m/03)), valid when $f(t)$ actually settles to a finite value. Its big advantage: you get $e_{ss}$ **without** inverting the Laplace transform. (The definition of $e_{ss}$ itself was introduced in [Module 02](#/m/02).)

**For the impulse input** <Src p="2" />:

Time domain — for $t>0$ the impulse is over, $r(t)=\delta(t)=0$, so

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(0-\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right)=0
$$

$s$-domain — *"ya da Laplace dönüşümü kullanarak"* ("or using the Laplace transform"), with $R(s)=1$:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\right)=0\cdot\left(1-1\right)=0
$$

Both routes give the same result, as the notes remark (*"sonucuna aynı şekilde ulaşırız"*).

## Unit step response

<Src p="2" /> *"Giriş fonksiyonu R(s) olarak birim basamak (1/s) fonksiyonu alınırsa, ters Laplace dönüşümü yaparak zaman bölgesi çözümünü elde ederiz."* — "If the <Term en="unit step" tr="birim basamak" /> $\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)$ is taken as the input, we obtain the <Term en="time-domain solution" tr="zaman bölgesi çözümü" /> by inverse Laplace transform."

The step is the most important test input: it models "switch on", "change the set-point", "apply a constant load".

### Derivation of the step response

<Src p="3" />

$$
Y(s)=R(s)T(s)=\frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau s+1}
$$

**Partial fractions.** Write $\dfrac{1}{s(\tau s+1)}=\dfrac{A}{s}+\dfrac{B}{\tau s+1}$. Cover-up: $A=\left.\dfrac{1}{\tau s+1}\right|_{s=0}=1$; $B=\left.\dfrac{1}{s}\right|_{s=-1/\tau}=-\tau$. Hence

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{\tau}{\tau s+1}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{s+\frac{1}{\tau}}
$$

(the last step divides numerator and denominator of the second term by $\tau$). Check by recombining: $\dfrac{(\tau s+1)-\tau s}{s(\tau s+1)}=\dfrac{1}{s(\tau s+1)}$ ✓. Inverting term by term ($\Li\{1/s\}=1$, $\Li\{1/(s+a)\}=e^{-at}$):

<KeyFormula title="Unit step response of 1/(τs+1)">

$$
y(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau},\qquad t\ge0
$$

</KeyFormula>

### The step response graph

<StepPlot />

<Src p="3" /> The notes draw the input level $r(t)=1$ as a dashed horizontal line, the rising curve, and a dashed tangent from the origin that reaches the level 1 exactly at $t=\tau$.

- **Start:** $y(0)=1-1=0$. The output cannot jump — the first-order system needs time.
- **Final value:** $y(\infty)=1$ = the input. Perfect tracking eventually.
- **Shape:** concave down: it rises fastest at the start (largest error) and then slows down.
- **Connection to the impulse response:** the step is the integral of the impulse, so the step response is the integral of the impulse response: $\int_0^t\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-u/\tau}du=1-e^{-t/\tau}$ ✓. Equivalently the slope of the step response is the impulse response.

### Time constant on the step curve

$$
y(\tau)=1-e^{-1}=1-0.368=0.632
$$

**At $t=\tau$ the step response has covered 63.2 % of the way to its final value.** This is the most common practical definition of the time constant.

**Initial slope** <Src p="3" />:

$$
\left.\frac{dy}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\left.\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{\tau}
$$

So the tangent at the origin is the line $y=\frac{t}{\tau}$, which reaches the final value 1 at $t=\tau$: *if the output kept rising at its initial speed, it would arrive in exactly one time constant.* In reality it has only reached 63.2 % by then.

| $t$ | $\tau$ | $2\tau$ | $3\tau$ | $4\tau$ | $5\tau$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau}$ | 0.632 | 0.865 | 0.950 | 0.982 | 0.993 |

### Settling time

<Src p="3" /> *"Sistemin yerleşme süresi yaklaşık 4τ–5τ civarındadır."* — "the system's <Term en="settling time" tr="yerleşme süresi" /> is approximately $4\tau$–$5\tau$."

Where do these numbers come from? Ask: when is the output within a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the final value, i.e. $e^{-t/\tau}=\varepsilon$?

$$
t=\tau\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
$$

- 2 % band ($\varepsilon=0.02$): $t=\tau\ln50=3.91\tau\approx4\tau$ → at $4\tau$ the output is at 98.2 %.
- 1 % band: $t=\tau\ln100=4.61\tau$; at $5\tau$ the output is at 99.3 %.
- 5 % band: $t=\tau\ln20=3.00\tau$.

<KeyFormula title="Settling time of a first-order system">

$$
t_s\approx4\tau\ (\pm2\%)\qquad\text{to}\qquad5\tau\ (\pm1\%)
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Tip title="Sketching the step response in an exam">

1. Draw the final-value line (here $y=1$; in general $y=K$, see Example 1).
2. Mark $\tau$ on the time axis and draw the tangent from the origin to the point $(\tau,\ \text{final value})$.
3. Mark the point $(\tau,\ 0.632\times\text{final value})$.
4. Mark $4\tau$ or $5\tau$, where the curve visibly merges with the final-value line.
5. Draw a smooth, concave-down curve starting along the tangent, through the 63 % point, flattening into the final line.

</Tip>

### Step steady-state error

<Src p="3" /> With $R(s)=\frac{1}{s}$:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\cdot\frac{1}{s}\right)=\lim_{s\to0}\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\right)=1-1=0
$$

Time domain check: $e(t)=1-(1-e^{-t/\tau})=e^{-t/\tau}\to0$ ✓. The integrator in the loop keeps pushing until the error is exactly zero — as predicted by $\tau\dot y=e$.

## Unit ramp response

<Src p="4" /> *"Giriş fonksiyonunun birim rampa (1/s²) fonksiyonu olması durumunda çıkış fonksiyonu şöyle bulunur"* — "when the input is the <Term en="unit ramp" tr="birim rampa" /> $\left(\frac{1}{s^2}\right)$, the output is found as follows." A ramp $r(t)=t$ models a target that moves at constant speed: an antenna tracking a satellite, a reference temperature that rises steadily.

### Partial fractions for the ramp

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau s+1}
$$

There is a **repeated** pole at $s=0$, so the expansion must contain both $\frac{1}{s^2}$ and $\frac{1}{s}$:

$$
\frac{1}{s^{2}(\tau s+1)}=\frac{A}{s^{2}}+\frac{B}{s}+\frac{C}{\tau s+1}
$$

Multiply both sides by $s^2(\tau s+1)$:

$$
1=A(\tau s+1)+Bs(\tau s+1)+Cs^{2}
$$

- $s=0$: $1=A$ → $A=1$.
- $s=-\frac{1}{\tau}$: $1=C\cdot\frac{1}{\tau^2}$ → $C=\tau^2$.
- Coefficient of $s^2$: $0=B\tau+C$ → $B=-\frac{C}{\tau}=-\tau$.
- (Check with the coefficient of $s$: $0=A\tau+B=\tau-\tau$ ✓.)

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}-\frac{\tau}{s}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\tau s+1}
$$

<Src p="4" /> For the last term, $\dfrac{\tau^2}{\tau s+1}=\dfrac{\tau}{s+\frac1\tau}$, whose inverse is $\tau e^{-t/\tau}$. With $\Li\{1/s^2\}=t$ and $\Li\{1/s\}=1$:

<KeyFormula title="Unit ramp response of 1/(τs+1)">

$$
y(t)=t-\tau+\tau e^{-t/\tau},\qquad t\ge0
$$

</KeyFormula>

Checks: $y(0)=0-\tau+\tau=0$ ✓. $\dot y(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau}$, so $\dot y(0)=0$ (the curve starts flat — visible in the notes' sketch) and $\dot y\to1$ (eventually it moves at the same speed as the input). Notice $\dot y$ equals the step response, and the step response is in turn the derivative of the ramp response — again because the ramp is the integral of the step.

### The constant lag tau

<RampPlot />

<Src p="4" /> The notes draw $r(t)$ dashed, $y(t)$ solid below it, and a small blue double arrow labelled $\tau$ between them.

- **Transient part:** $\tau e^{-t/\tau}$ — dies out after about $4\tau$–$5\tau$.
- **Steady-state part:** $y_{ss}(t)=t-\tau$ — a line **parallel** to the input (same slope 1) but shifted down by $\tau$.

*"Görüldüğü gibi y(t) çıkışı r(t) girişini kalıcı duruma ulaştıktan sonra da τ farkı ile izlemektedir."* — "the output follows the input with a difference of $\tau$ even after reaching steady state."

Because both lines have slope 1, the gap can be read in two ways: the output is $\tau$ **lower** (vertical gap) and also $\tau$ seconds **late** (horizontal gap: $y_{ss}(t)=r(t-\tau)$). A system with a small time constant follows a moving target closely.

### Ramp steady-state error

<Src p="5" /> Time domain:

$$
e(t)=r(t)-y(t)=t-\left(t-\tau+\tau e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right)=\tau-\tau e^{-t/\tau}\;\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\;\tau
$$

$s$-domain:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}-\frac{1}{\tau s+1}\cdot\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)=\lim_{s\to0}s\,\frac{\tau s+1-1}{s^{2}(\tau s+1)}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{\tau s^2}{s^2(\tau s+1)}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{\tau}{\tau s+1}=\tau
$$

(The notes do the same cancellation by crossing out $s$, $+1-1$ and $s^2$.)

<KeyFormula title="Ramp steady-state error of the standard loop">

$$
e_{ss}=\tau
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Why a constant error and not zero?** From $\tau\dot y=e$: to keep up with a target moving at speed 1, the output needs $\dot y=1$, and the only way the integrator produces that speed is to be fed a constant error $e=\tau$. Zero error would mean zero speed, and the output would fall behind. So a constant lag is the price of tracking a ramp with this loop.

## Summary of the three responses

| Input $r(t)$ | $R(s)$ | Output $y(t)$ of $\frac{1}{\tau s+1}$ | Start | Final behaviour | $e_{ss}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\delta(t)$ | $1$ | $\frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}$ | $\frac1\tau$, slope $-\frac{1}{\tau^2}$ | $\to0$ | $0$ |
| $u(t)$ | $\frac1s$ | $1-e^{-t/\tau}$ | $0$, slope $\frac1\tau$ | $\to1$ | $0$ |
| $t$ | $\frac{1}{s^2}$ | $t-\tau+\tau e^{-t/\tau}$ | $0$, slope $0$ | $\to t-\tau$ | $\tau$ |

Each response is the derivative of the one below it, just as each input is the derivative of the one below it. One number, $\tau$, controls all three.

## Explore tau and K

The first slider changes $\tau$, the second the DC gain $K$ of the more general form $T(s)=\frac{K}{\tau s+1}$ that Example 1 introduces below (keep $K=1$ for the standard loop). Watch:

- the pole at $-1/\tau$ moves left as $\tau$ shrinks, and all three curves get faster;
- the tangents always hit their targets at $t=\tau$;
- with $K\ne1$ the step response settles at $K$ instead of 1 (step error $1-K$), and the ramp response no longer stays parallel to the input (ramp error infinite).

## Example 1 with DC gain below one

<Ex1LoopBD />

### The gain form of a first-order TF

A real loop rarely gives exactly $\frac{1}{\tau s+1}$. Any first-order transfer function without a zero can be brought to the **gain–time-constant form**

$$
T(s)=\frac{b}{s+a}=\frac{b/a}{\frac{1}{a}s+1}=\frac{K}{\tau s+1},\qquad K=\frac{b}{a},\ \ \tau=\frac{1}{a}
$$

The trick: **make the constant term of the denominator equal to 1** by dividing numerator and denominator by $a$.

- $\tau=\frac1a$ is the time constant (the pole is $-a=-\frac{1}{\tau}$).
- $K=T(0)$ is the <Term en="DC gain" tr="DC kazanç" /> (steady-state gain): what a constant input of 1 produces in steady state, since a constant corresponds to $s\to0$.

Because the system is linear, multiplying $T$ by $K$ just multiplies every response by $K$:

$$
y_\delta(t)=\frac{K}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau},\qquad y_{step}(t)=K\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad y_{ramp}(t)=K\left(t-\tau+\tau e^{-t/\tau}\right)
$$

<KeyFormula title="General first-order system (gain form)">

$$
T(s)=\frac{K}{\tau s+1},\qquad y_{step}(t)=K\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad e_{ss,step}=1-K
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Example title="Example 1: closed-loop transfer function of G = 4/(s+1)" src="5">

**Given:** unity negative feedback around $G(s)=\dfrac{4}{s+1}$. **Asked:** $T(s)$, its $\tau$ and DC gain.

<Step title="Apply T = G/(1+G)">

$$
T(s)=\frac{\dfrac{4}{s+1}}{1+\dfrac{4}{s+1}}
$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $(s+1)$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{4}{(s+1)+4}=\frac{4}{s+5}
$$

</Step>

<Step title="Bring to the gain form">

Divide numerator and denominator by 5:

$$
T(s)=\frac{4/5}{\frac{1}{5}s+1}=\frac{0.8}{0.2s+1}
$$

(the notes write this with decimal commas, $\frac{0{,}8}{0{,}2s+1}$).

</Step>

<Step title="Read the parameters">

$K=0.8$, $\tau=0.2$ s, closed-loop pole at $s=-5$.

</Step>

<Answer>

$$
T(s)=\frac{4}{s+5}=\frac{0.8}{0.2s+1},\qquad K=0.8,\ \ \tau=0.2\ \text{s}
$$

</Answer>

**Interpretation:** the plant alone, $\frac{4}{s+1}$, has time constant 1 s (pole at $-1$). Feedback moved the pole to $-5$: the closed loop is **5 times faster**. The price: the DC gain is 0.8, not 1, so a step will not be tracked perfectly. Unlike the standard loop, $G=\frac{4}{s+1}$ contains **no integrator** (no pure $\frac1s$), so nothing forces the error to zero.

### Initial and final value theorems

Example 1 is the first place the notes use both theorems ([Module 03](#/m/03)) to check a response without inverting it:

<KeyFormula title="Initial and final value theorems">

$$
y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}sY(s),\qquad y(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}sY(s)
$$

</KeyFormula>

- **Initial value theorem** (<Term en="initial value theorem" tr="ilk değer teoremi" />): needs $Y(s)$ **strictly proper** (numerator degree lower than denominator degree), otherwise $y(t)$ contains an impulse at $t=0$ and the limit diverges — see Example 2.
- **Final value theorem** (<Term en="final value" tr="son değer" />): needs $y(t)$ to actually settle, i.e. all poles of $sY(s)$ in the left half-plane. If $y(t)$ grows (like a ramp response), $\lim_{s\to0}sY(s)=\infty$ just tells you "it grows without bound".
- The notes write $y(0)$; strictly it is $y(0^+)$, the value just after the impulse. Before $t=0$ the output is 0, so the impulse response jumps from 0 to 4 at $t=0$.

### Example 1 ramp response

<Example title="Example 1: unit ramp response" src="7">

**Given:** $T(s)=\dfrac{4}{s+5}$, $R(s)=\dfrac{1}{s^2}$. **Asked:** $y(t)$, $y_{ss}(t)$, $e_{ss}$.

<Step title="Partial fractions">

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\cdot\frac{4}{s+5}=\frac{A}{s^{2}}+\frac{B}{s}+\frac{C}{s+5}
$$

Multiply by $s^2(s+5)$: $4=A(s+5)+Bs(s+5)+Cs^2$.

- $s=0$: $4=5A$ → $A=0.8$.
- $s=-5$: $4=25C$ → $C=0.16$.
- coefficient of $s^2$: $0=B+C$ → $B=-0.16$.
- check, coefficient of $s$: $0=A+5B=0.8-0.8$ ✓.

$$
Y(s)=\frac{0.8}{s^{2}}+\frac{-0.16}{s}+\frac{0.16}{s+5}
$$

</Step>

<Step title="Invert">

$$
y(t)=0.8t-0.16+0.16e^{-5t}
$$

Check: $y(0)=-0.16+0.16=0$ ✓. Same as $K(t-\tau+\tau e^{-t/\tau})$ with $K=0.8$, $\tau=0.2$: $0.8t-0.16+0.16e^{-5t}$ ✓.

</Step>

<Step title="Steady-state output">

*"$t>5\tau$ (1 s) olduğunda $0{,}16e^{-5t}$ terimi ihmal edilebilir"* — for $t>5\tau=1$ s the exponential <Term en="can be neglected" tr="ihmal edilebilir" />:

$$
y_{ss}(t)=0.8t-0.16
$$

</Step>

<Step title="Steady-state error, time domain">

$$
e_{ss}(t)=t-\left(0.8t-0.16\right)=0.2t+0.16
$$

This grows without bound.

</Step>

<Step title="Steady-state error, s-domain">

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}-\frac{4}{s^{2}(s+5)}\right)=\lim_{s\to0}s\,\frac{s+5-4}{s^{2}(s+5)}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s+1}{s(s+5)}=\infty
$$

</Step>

<Answer>

$$
y(t)=0.8t-0.16+0.16e^{-5t},\qquad e(t)\to0.2t+0.16\to\infty
$$

</Answer>

**Why infinite?** In steady state the output rises with slope 0.8 (the DC gain), while the input rises with slope 1. Two lines with different slopes drift apart: every second the output falls another 0.2 behind. Compare with the standard loop, where $K=1$ gave equal slopes and a constant gap $\tau$. A system that cannot even hold a constant (step error 0.2) has no chance of following a ramp.

</Example>

### Biproper transfer functions

<Example title="Example 2: closed-loop transfer function of G = s/(s+1)" src="8">

**Given:** unity negative feedback around $G(s)=\dfrac{s}{s+1}$. **Asked:** $T(s)$ and a form suitable for inversion.

<Step title="Apply T = G/(1+G)">

$$
T(s)=\frac{\dfrac{s}{s+1}}{1+\dfrac{s}{s+1}}=\frac{s}{(s+1)+s}=\frac{s}{2s+1}=\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}
$$

(the last form divides numerator and denominator by 2).

</Step>

<Step title="Long division (numerator degree = denominator degree)">

Write $0.5s=0.5(s+0.5)-0.25$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{0.5(s+0.5)-0.25}{s+0.5}=0.5-\frac{0.25}{s+0.5}
$$

Check: $\frac{0.5s+0.25-0.25}{s+0.5}=\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}$ ✓.

</Step>

<Answer>

$$
T(s)=\frac{s}{2s+1}=\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}=0.5-\frac{0.25}{s+0.5},\qquad \tau=2\ \text{s}
$$

</Answer>

</Example>

Here the numerator and denominator have the **same degree** (both 1). Such a transfer function is called **biproper**: proper (numerator degree not larger than denominator degree) but not *strictly* proper. Consequences:

- $T(\infty)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{s}{2s+1}=0.5\ne0$: part of the input passes **straight through** to the output instantly (a "direct feed-through" of 0.5). At very high frequencies $G\to1$, so $T\to\frac{1}{1+1}=0.5$.
- $T(0)=0$: the DC gain is zero. The $s$ in the numerator of $G$ is a **zero at the origin** — a differentiator — so constants are blocked completely.
- The pole is still at $-0.5$, so the time constant is $\tau=\frac{1}{0.5}=2$ s (also visible from $\frac{s}{2s+1}$: $\tau s+1$ with $\tau=2$).

### Initial value theorem with an impulse

<Src p="9" /> *"Bu fonksiyonda görüldüğü gibi ilk değer teoremini uygulamak sorunludur. Sıfırdan hemen sonraki an için t=0⁺ fonksiyonun alacağı değer sonludur, hesaplanabilir."* — "As seen in this function, applying the initial value theorem is **problematic**. For the instant just after zero, $t=0^+$, the value the function takes is <Term en="finite" tr="sonlu" /> and can be calculated."

**What exactly is the problem?** Apply the theorem literally to the whole $Y(s)$:

$$
\lim_{s\to\infty}sY(s)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\left(0.5s-\frac{0.25s}{s+0.5}\right)=\infty-0.25=\infty
$$

The term $0.5s$ blows up. This is no accident: the constant $0.5$ in $Y(s)$ **is** the impulse $0.5\delta(t)$, and at $t=0$ an impulse is "infinite". The initial value theorem is derived for functions without impulses at the origin, i.e. for strictly proper $Y(s)$.

**The fix (what the notes do implicitly):**

1. Split $Y(s)$ by long division into constant + strictly proper remainder: $Y(s)=0.5+Y_r(s)$, with $Y_r(s)=-\dfrac{0.25}{s+0.5}$.
2. The constant is the impulse; it acts only at $t=0$ and is gone at $t=0^+$.
3. Apply the theorem to $Y_r$ only: $y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}sY_r(s)=-0.25$.

The notes' line $y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\left(0.5-\frac{0.25}{s+0.5}\right)=\left.\frac{-0.25s}{s}\right|_{s\to\infty}=-0.25$ silently drops the $0.5s$ term — which is exactly step 3. Equivalently, for a biproper $Y(s)$ you can use $y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\big[Y(s)-Y(\infty)\big]$.

<Uncertain title="How to read the p. 9 initial value computation">

As written, $\lim_{s\to\infty}s\left(0.5-\frac{0.25}{s+0.5}\right)$ diverges because of $0.5s$. The result $y(0^+)=-0.25$ is nevertheless correct for the non-impulsive part of $y(t)$ and agrees with $-0.25e^{0}$. In an exam, write the split explicitly (step 1 above) so that your working is mathematically correct.

</Uncertain>

The final value theorem has no such problem: $Y(s)=\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}$ has its only pole at $-0.5$, $sY(s)\to0$ as $s\to0$, and $y(\infty)=0$ <Src p="9" />.

### Open-loop versus closed-loop time constant

<GeneralLoopBD />

For a unity loop around $G(s)=\dfrac{K}{s+a}$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{K}{s+a+K}=\frac{\frac{K}{a+K}}{\frac{1}{a+K}s+1},\qquad \tau_{CL}=\frac{1}{a+K},\qquad K_{DC}=\frac{K}{a+K},\qquad e_{ss,step}=\frac{a}{a+K}

$T(s)=\frac{6}{s+8}=\frac{0.75}{0.125s+1}$, $e_{ss}=1-0.75=0.25$.

</Step>

<Answer>

$K=6$, $T(s)=\frac{6}{s+8}$, $e_{ss,step}=0.25$.

</Answer>

**Interpretation:** a larger $K$ buys both speed and accuracy in this loop; with no integrator, the step error is never exactly zero.

</Example>

### Reading tau from a measured graph

<MeasuredStepPlot />

<Example title="Reading tau from a measured step response">

**Given:** the measured unit step response above. The output settles at 1.6. **Asked:** $\tau$, $K$ and the transfer function, by two methods.

<Step title="Final value gives K">

The input is a unit step, the output settles at $1.6$ → $K=1.6$.

</Step>

<Step title="Method 1: the 63.2 % point">

$0.632\times1.6=1.011$. Draw a horizontal line at 1.011 and read where the curve crosses it: $t\approx0.75$ s. So $\tau\approx0.75$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Method 2: the tangent at the origin">

The tangent at $t=0$ has slope $K/\tau$ and reaches the final value at $t=\tau$. Draw the tangent through the first samples and see where it hits $y=1.6$: at $t\approx0.75$ s. (Numerically: initial slope $\approx\frac{1.6}{0.75}=2.13$ per second.)

</Step>

<Answer>

$$
T(s)\approx\frac{1.6}{0.75s+1}
$$

</Answer>

**Which method in practice?** The 63.2 % method is more reliable: drawing a tangent at the very start of a noisy measured curve is hard, and a small error in its slope shifts its intersection noticeably. </Example>

## Edge cases

- **$\tau\to0$:** $T(s)\to1$. The pole goes to $-\infty$, the step response becomes an instant jump, the ramp lag $\tau$ vanishes and the impulse response $\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}$ becomes an impulse itself. An ideal, infinitely fast system.
- **Large $\tau$:** pole near the origin, sluggish response, large ramp lag.
- **$K\ne1$ (Example 1):** the step is not tracked exactly ($e_{ss}=1-K$) and the ramp error is infinite, because output and input slopes differ. $K>1$ gives negative step error and $-\infty$ ramp error.
- **Zero at the origin, biproper $T(s)$ (Example 2):** impulse in the impulse response, a jump at $t=0^+$ in the step response, and the initial value theorem must be applied to the strictly proper part.
- **Impulse input:** $e_{ss}=0$ for every stable system, because both input and output vanish after the transient — the impulse error tells you little about accuracy; the step and ramp errors do.
- **FVT on a growing signal:** $\lim_{s\to0}s(\ldots)=\infty$ only says "unbounded". It cannot give you the steady-state *function* $y_{ss}(t)=0.8t-0.16$ — for that you need the time-domain expression.

<Example title="Example 2 driven by a unit step">

**Given:** $T(s)=\dfrac{0.5s}{s+0.5}$ (Example 2), unit step input. **Asked:** $y(t)$, $y(0^+)$, $e_{ss}$.

<Step title="Output">

$$
Y(s)=\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}\cdot\frac{1}{s}=\frac{0.5}{s+0.5}\;\Rightarrow\;y(t)=0.5e^{-0.5t}
$$

The $s$ cancels: this $Y(s)$ is strictly proper, so there is no impulse.

</Step>

<Step title="Initial value — now the theorem works directly">

$y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{0.5s}{s+0.5}=0.5$. The output **jumps** to 0.5 at $t=0$ — the direct feed-through of half the step.

</Step>

<Step title="Steady-state error">

$e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac1s-\frac{0.5}{s+0.5}\right)=1-0=1$.

</Step>

<Answer>

$y(t)=0.5e^{-0.5t}$, $y(0^+)=0.5$, $y(\infty)=0$, $e_{ss}=1$.

</Answer>

**Interpretation:** a loop with a differentiating plant ($G=\frac{s}{s+1}$, DC gain 0) cannot hold any constant output at all: the step is "forgotten" with time constant 2 s and the full step remains as error.

</Example>

## How to solve exam problems

<Procedure title="First-order time-response problem">

1. **Reduce the loop.** If a block diagram is given, compute $T(s)=\frac{G}{1+G}$ (unity negative feedback). Simplify to a single fraction.
2. **Check the type.** First-order denominator? Numerator a constant → gain form $\frac{K}{\tau s+1}$. Numerator contains $s$ → biproper, do long division first (Example 2).
3. **Normalise.** Divide by the constant term of the denominator: $\frac{b}{s+a}=\frac{b/a}{(1/a)s+1}$. Read $\tau=\frac1a$, $K=\frac ba$, pole $-a$.
4. **Write the response** for the given input: $R(s)=1,\ \frac1s,\ \frac{1}{s^2}$; partial fractions; invert. Or use the ready forms $\frac K\tau e^{-t/\tau}$, $K(1-e^{-t/\tau})$, $K(t-\tau+\tau e^{-t/\tau})$.
5. **Characteristic numbers:** $y(\tau)=0.632K$ (step), initial slopes $\pm K/\tau$ or $-K/\tau^2$, settling time $4\tau$–$5\tau$.
6. **Steady-state error:** $e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s[R(s)-Y(s)]$; confirm with $\lim_{t\to\infty}[r(t)-y(t)]$.
7. **Verify:** IVT ($s\to\infty$) against $y(0^+)$, FVT ($s\to0$) against $y(\infty)$; recombine partial fractions.
8. **Sketch:** final-value line, tangent to $(\tau,\text{final})$, 63 % point, $4\tau$–$5\tau$ mark.

</Procedure>

<Mistake title="Using the open-loop time constant">

For $G=\frac{4}{s+1}$ the plant has $\tau=1$ s, but the closed loop has $\tau=0.2$ s. Read $\tau$ from $T(s)$, not from $G(s)$.

</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Reading tau before normalising">

$\frac{4}{s+5}$ does **not** have $\tau=5$ or $\tau=4$. Divide by 5: $\frac{0.8}{0.2s+1}$, so $\tau=0.2$ s $=1/(\text{pole magnitude})$.

</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Mixing up the limits">

Initial value: $s\to\infty$. Final value and $e_{ss}$: $s\to0$. (The notes themselves slip once on p. 6.)

</Mistake>

<Mistake title="63.2 percent of the wrong value">

For $K\ne1$ the step response reaches $0.632K$ at $t=\tau$, not $0.632$. Example 1: $y(0.2)=0.506$.

</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Applying the FVT where it does not apply">

The FVT gives a single number $\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)$ and only when that limit exists. For a ramp response $y(t)=0.8t-0.16+\ldots$ there is no finite limit; $\lim_{s\to0}sY(s)=\infty$. To get the steady-state behaviour $0.2t+0.16$ of the error you must use the time-domain expression. Likewise, never apply the IVT to a $Y(s)$ that contains a constant term without removing it first.

</Mistake>

<Tip title="Fast answers to memorise">

For $T(s)=\frac{K}{\tau s+1}$ with unit inputs: impulse $e_{ss}=0$; step $e_{ss}=1-K$; ramp $e_{ss}=\tau$ if $K=1$, otherwise $\pm\infty$. For a unity loop, $K=1$ exactly when $G(s)$ contains an integrator $\frac1s$ (like $\frac{1}{\tau s}$).

</Tip>

<Exercise title="What happens as tau goes to zero" kind="edge" level={2}>

Describe what happens to the pole, the step response, the impulse response and the ramp error of $\frac{1}{\tau s+1}$ as $\tau\to0$. Is this physically achievable?

<Solution>
