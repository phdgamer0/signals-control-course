## What is this about?

Push a car down at one corner and let go. Depending on how worn the shock absorbers are, the body either sinks back slowly without bouncing, returns as fast as possible without bouncing, or bobs up and down a few times before settling. Three very different behaviours — yet all three come from **one** equation with only two knobs: how "stiff and quick" the system is, and how much "friction" (damping) it has.

That is exactly what a <Term en="second-order control system" tr="ikinci derece kontrol sistemi" /> is. Its output obeys a differential equation with a second derivative (a mass with a spring and a damper, an RLC circuit, a motor driving a load, a position servo in a feedback loop). In the previous module the first-order system could only rise smoothly like $1-e^{-t/\tau}$. A second-order system can also **overshoot and oscillate**, and that is what makes it both more useful (it can be fast) and more dangerous (it can ring).

In this module the professor takes the standard second-order loop and works out, for every combination of

- **input**: unit impulse, unit step, unit ramp, and
- **damping case**: overdamped, critically damped, underdamped,

the exact time response $y(t)$ (by partial fractions and the inverse Laplace transform) and the <Term en="steady-state error" tr="kalıcı durum hatası" />. These nine derivations are the core of the exam.

Two numbers describe everything:

- $\omega_n$ — the <Term en="natural frequency" tr="doğal frekans" /> in rad/s: how fast the system wants to move (how stiff the spring is relative to the mass).
- $\xi$ — the <Term en="damping factor" tr="sönüm faktörü" /> (dimensionless; English books call it the damping ratio and often write $\zeta$): how strongly the motion is braked.

**After this module you can:**

- derive $T(s)=\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}$ from the loop and read $\omega_n$, $\xi$, $\alpha$, $\omega$, $\theta$ and the roots off any second-order transfer function (also with a DC gain different from 1);
- decide whether a system is overdamped, critically damped or underdamped and say what that means physically;
- derive the impulse, step and ramp responses in all three cases with partial fractions (distinct, repeated and complex roots);
- convert $\cos\omega t+\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\sin\omega t$ into a single shifted sine, and know why the ramp response has phase $2\theta$;
- compute steady-state errors with $e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s[R(s)-Y(s)]$;
- sketch every response by hand, with the right start, slope and final behaviour.

## The standard second-order system

The professor starts from this loop <Src p="1" />:

<StandardLoopBD />

- $R(s)$ is the reference (input), $Y(s)$ the output, $E(s)=R(s)-Y(s)$ the error leaving the summing junction.
- The forward block $G(s)=\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+2\xi\omega_n)}$ is the "plant plus controller". The factor $\frac1s$ is an integrator (for a motor: speed integrates to position); $\frac{1}{s+2\xi\omega_n}$ is a lag.
- The feedback is unity (the output itself is subtracted), with a minus sign.

<Def term="Natural frequency" tr="doğal frekans">

$\omega_n$ (rad/s) is the <Term en="natural frequency of the system" tr="sistemin doğal frekansı" />: the frequency at which the system would oscillate forever if there were no damping at all ($\xi=0$). <Src p="1" />

</Def>

<Def term="Damping factor" tr="sönüm faktörü">

$\xi$ is the <Term en="damping factor of the system" tr="sistemin sönüm faktörü" />. It measures how much the motion is braked: $\xi=0$ no braking (endless oscillation), small $\xi$ light braking (decaying oscillation), $\xi=1$ just enough braking to prevent any oscillation, $\xi>1$ heavy braking (sluggish). <Src p="1" />

</Def>

### Deriving the closed-loop transfer function

The notes simply state the <Term en="general transfer function" tr="genel transfer fonksiyonu" />; here is where it comes from. Around the loop:

$$
E(s)=R(s)-Y(s),\qquad Y(s)=G(s)E(s)
$$

Substitute the first into the second and collect $Y(s)$:

$$
Y(s)=G(s)R(s)-G(s)Y(s)\ \Longrightarrow\ Y(s)\big[1+G(s)\big]=G(s)R(s)\ \Longrightarrow\ T(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{G(s)}{1+G(s)}
$$

Now insert $G(s)=\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+2\xi\omega_n)}$ and multiply numerator and denominator by $s(s+2\xi\omega_n)$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+2\xi\omega_n)}}{1+\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+2\xi\omega_n)}}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+2\xi\omega_n)+\omega_n^2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}
$$

<KeyFormula title="Standard second-order closed-loop transfer function (p. 1)">

$$
T(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Src p="1" /> Two things to notice:

- **DC gain is 1.** At $s=0$: $T(0)=\omega_n^2/\omega_n^2=1$. A constant input is eventually reproduced exactly at the output. (This comes from the integrator $1/s$ in the loop.)
- The **pattern** to recognise in an exam: constant term $=\omega_n^2$, coefficient of $s$ $=2\xi\omega_n$, coefficient of $s^2$ $=1$. If a transfer function is not normalised (leading coefficient not 1), divide through first.

### The differential equation behind T(s)

Cross-multiplying $Y(s)(s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2)=\omega_n^2R(s)$ and reading $s\leftrightarrow\frac{d}{dt}$ (zero initial conditions):

$$
\ddot y(t)+2\xi\omega_n\,\dot y(t)+\omega_n^2\,y(t)=\omega_n^2\,r(t)
$$

This is the equation every response in this module solves. (All responses below were also checked by solving this ODE numerically.)

### Physical intuition: mass-spring-damper and RLC

<Intuition>

A mass $m$ on a spring $k$ with a damper $b$, whose spring end is moved by $r(t)$: $m\ddot y+b\dot y+ky=k\,r$. Divide by $m$:

$$
\ddot y+\frac{b}{m}\dot y+\frac{k}{m}y=\frac{k}{m}r\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_n^2=\frac km,\qquad 2\xi\omega_n=\frac bm\ \Rightarrow\ \xi=\frac{b}{2\sqrt{km}}
$$

Stiffer spring or lighter mass → higher $\omega_n$ (faster). More friction → higher $\xi$ (less bounce). The car-suspension story from the introduction is exactly this.

</Intuition>

The same equation appears in electric circuits (the RLC circuits of [Module 05](#/m/05)):

<SeriesRLC />

KVL around the loop with $i=C\dot v_o$: $v_i=Ri+L\dot i+v_o=RC\dot v_o+LC\ddot v_o+v_o$, so

$$
\ddot v_o+\frac RL\dot v_o+\frac1{LC}v_o=\frac1{LC}v_i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\omega_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad \xi=\frac R2\sqrt{\frac CL}
$$

The resistor is the damper: $R=0$ gives an undamped LC oscillator, a large $R$ gives a sluggish, non-oscillating response.

### Roots of the characteristic equation

The behaviour is decided by the roots (poles) of the denominator $s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0$ — the <Term en="roots of the system" tr="sistemin kökleri" />. Quadratic formula with $a=1$, $b=2\xi\omega_n$, $c=\omega_n^2$:

$$
s_{1,2}=\frac{-2\xi\omega_n\pm\sqrt{4\xi^2\omega_n^2-4\omega_n^2}}{2}=-\xi\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}
$$

<KeyFormula title="System roots (p. 1)">

$$
s_{1,2}=-\xi\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Src p="1" /> Everything hinges on the sign of $\xi^2-1$ under the square root.

## Three damping cases

The system is treated in three different ways depending on the damping factor <Src p="2" />:

| Case | Turkish | Condition | Roots | Typical motion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overdamped | aşırı sönümlü | $\xi>1$ | real and distinct (gerçek ve ayrık): $-p_1,\,-p_2$ | slow, no overshoot |
| Critically damped | kritik sönümlü | $\xi=1$ | real and repeated (gerçek ve katlı): $-\omega_n,\,-\omega_n$ | fastest without overshoot |
| Underdamped | düşük sönümlü | $\xi<1$ | complex conjugate (karmaşık ve eşlenik): $-\alpha\pm j\omega$ | decaying oscillation |

### Overdamped case

<Def term="Overdamped system" tr="aşırı sönümlü sistem">

If $\xi>1$ then $\xi^2-1>0$, the square root is real, and the roots are **real and distinct**. <Src p="2" /> The professor names them $s_1=-p_1$, $s_2=-p_2$ with <Src p="4" />

$$
p_1=\xi\omega_n-\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1},\qquad p_2=\xi\omega_n+\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}
$$

</Def>

Both $p_1$ and $p_2$ are positive (because $\sqrt{\xi^2-1}<\xi$), so both roots lie on the negative real axis; $p_1$ is the smaller one — the **slow** root that dominates the response. Two handy facts (Vieta's formulas for the quadratic) used again and again below:

$$
p_1p_2=\omega_n^2,\qquad p_1+p_2=2\xi\omega_n,\qquad p_2-p_1=2\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}
$$

### Critically damped case

<Def term="Critically damped system" tr="kritik sönümlü sistem">

If $\xi=1$ the square root vanishes and the roots are **real and repeated**: $s_{1,2}=-\omega_n$. <Src p="2" /> The denominator is a perfect square: $s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=(s+\omega_n)^2$.

</Def>

### Underdamped case

<Def term="Underdamped system" tr="düşük sönümlü sistem">

If $\xi<1$ then $\xi^2-1<0$; write $\sqrt{\xi^2-1}=j\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$. The roots are **complex conjugate** <Src p="2" />:

$$
s_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm j\omega=-\xi\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2},\qquad \alpha=\xi\omega_n,\quad \omega=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}
$$

$\alpha$ is the decay rate (real part), $\omega$ the frequency at which the response actually oscillates (the damped frequency; the professor writes plain $\omega$, many books write $\omega_d$).

</Def>

<Note title="Strictly, 0 less than ξ less than 1">

The notes say "$\xi<1$". The formulas really need $0<\xi<1$: at $\xi=0$ the roots sit on the imaginary axis (pure oscillation, covered as a limit in [Edge cases](#/m/07/undamped-limit)), and $\xi<0$ would put them in the right half-plane (unstable). In this course $\xi$ is a damping, so it is non-negative.

</Note>

### Geometry of the complex roots

The underdamped root $-\alpha+j\omega$ has distance from the origin

$$
\sqrt{\alpha^2+\omega^2}=\sqrt{\xi^2\omega_n^2+\omega_n^2(1-\xi^2)}=\omega_n
$$

so the poles lie on a circle of radius $\omega_n$. The angle $\theta$ between the ray to the pole and the **negative** real axis satisfies

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\alpha}{\omega_n}=\xi,\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{\omega}{\omega_n}=\sqrt{1-\xi^2},\qquad \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}
$$

This is the same $\theta$ the professor introduces on p. 9 for the step response. In polar form the roots are $-\alpha\pm j\omega=-\omega_n e^{\mp j\theta}$, a fact we will use to see where the phase $2\theta$ in the ramp response comes from.

<SPlane poles={[{ re: -2, im: 3.464, label: '-\\alpha+j\\omega' }, { re: -2, im: -3.464, label: '-\\alpha-j\\omega' }]} lines={[{ re: -2, im: 3.464, dash: true }, { re: -2, im: -3.464, dash: true }]} range={5} imRange={5} caption="ξ = 0.5, ω_n = 4: poles at −2 ± j3.464, distance ω_n = 4 from the origin, angle θ = 60° from the negative real axis (cos θ = ξ)." />

<Intuition>

Moving along the circle (fixed $\omega_n$) from the real axis towards the imaginary axis lowers $\xi=\cos\theta$: the poles move right (slower decay $\alpha$) and up (faster oscillation $\omega$). At $\theta=90^\circ$ ($\xi=0$) there is no decay at all. At $\theta=0$ ($\xi=1$) the two poles meet on the real axis — critical damping — and for $\xi>1$ they split along the real axis, one towards the origin ($-p_1$, slow) and one away ($-p_2$, fast).

</Intuition>

### Example 1: identifying the parameters

<Example1BD />

<Example title="Example 1: type and parameters of the system" src="2–3">

**Given:** unity negative feedback around $G(s)=\dfrac{2}{s^2+2s+2}$. **Asked:** the type (damping case) and the parameters of the system (<Term en="type and parameters" tr="türü ve parametreleri" />).

**Method:** the type is a property of the **closed loop**, so first find $T(s)$, then match its denominator with $s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2$.

<Step title="Closed-loop transfer function">

$$
T(s)=\frac{G}{1+G}=\frac{\dfrac{2}{s^2+2s+2}}{1+\dfrac{2}{s^2+2s+2}}=\frac{2}{s^2+2s+2+2}=\frac{2}{s^2+2s+4}
$$

(Multiply top and bottom by $s^2+2s+2$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Match the denominator">

$$
\omega_n^2=4\Rightarrow\omega_n=2\ \text{rad/s},\qquad 2\xi\omega_n=2\Rightarrow\xi=\frac{2}{2\cdot2}=0.5
$$

(The notes write 0,5 with a decimal comma.)

</Step>

<Step title="Root parameters">

$$
\alpha=\xi\omega_n=0.5\cdot2=1,\qquad \omega=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}=2\sqrt{0.75}=\sqrt3=1.732
$$

$$
s_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm j\omega=-1\pm j1.732
$$

</Step>

<Step title="Type">

$\xi=0.5<1$ → complex-conjugate roots → **underdamped** (düşük sönümlü). The notes leave this word implicit.

</Step>

<Answer>

$T(s)=\dfrac{2}{s^2+2s+4}$, $\omega_n=2$ rad/s, $\xi=0.5$, $\alpha=1$, $\omega=1.732$ rad/s, $s_{1,2}=-1\pm j1.732$: an underdamped system. ($\theta=\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{0.75}/0.5)=60^\circ$.)

</Answer>

**Sanity check:** $(s+1)^2+3=s^2+2s+4$ ✓. Also $|s_1|=\sqrt{1+3}=2=\omega_n$ ✓.

</Example>

<Note title="The numerator is not ω_n² here">

$T(s)=\dfrac{2}{s^2+2s+4}=0.5\cdot\dfrac{4}{s^2+2s+4}$. The numerator is 2, not $\omega_n^2=4$, so this closed loop has DC gain $T(0)=0.5$: it is the standard form **times 0.5**. The notes do not mention it, but it matters as soon as you compute responses: every response of this system is 0.5 times the standard one, and the step settles at 0.5, not 1. The reason is that $G(s)$ here has no integrator (no factor $1/s$). Example 2 handles exactly this situation.

</Note>

## Partial fraction toolkit

Every response below is found the same way: $Y(s)=T(s)R(s)$, split into <Term en="partial fractions" tr="basit kesirler" />, and invert term by term with the <Term en="inverse Laplace transform" tr="ters Laplace dönüşümü" /> ([Module 03](#/m/03)). The professor uses three coefficient-finding rules (<Term en="coefficient-finding methods" tr="katsayı bulma yöntemleri" />):

### Distinct real roots

For a simple factor $(s+p)$ in the denominator:

$$
\frac{K}{s+p}\quad\text{with}\quad K=\lim_{s\to-p}\big[(s+p)Y(s)\big]\qquad\xrightarrow{\ \Li\ }\qquad Ke^{-pt}
$$

Multiply by the factor, cancel, then substitute the root ("cover-up" rule).

### Repeated roots

For a double factor $(s+p)^2$ there are two terms:

$$
\frac{K_a}{(s+p)^2}+\frac{K_b}{s+p},\qquad K_a=\lim_{s\to-p}\big[(s+p)^2Y(s)\big],\quad K_b=\lim_{s\to-p}\frac{d}{ds}\big[(s+p)^2Y(s)\big]
$$

and $\Li\left\{\dfrac{1}{(s+p)^2}\right\}=te^{-pt}$. The same rule applies to $s^2$ (a double root at $s=0$, $p=0$): $\dfrac{K_1}{s^2}+\dfrac{K_2}{s}$ with $K_1=\lim s^2Y$, $K_2=\lim \frac{d}{ds}[s^2Y]$, and $\Li\{1/s^2\}=t$.

Why the derivative? If $(s+p)^2Y(s)=K_a+K_b(s+p)+(s+p)^2(\dots)$, then at $s=-p$ the value gives $K_a$ and the first derivative gives $K_b$.

### Complex-conjugate roots (K and K star)

The professor writes $p_1=\alpha+j\omega$, $p_2=\alpha-j\omega$, so $(s+p_1)(s+p_2)=(s+\alpha)^2+\omega^2$ and the roots are $-p_1=-\alpha-j\omega$, $-p_2=-\alpha+j\omega$. Because $Y(s)$ has real coefficients, the two coefficients are complex conjugates:

$$
\frac{K}{s+\alpha+j\omega}+\frac{K^*}{s+\alpha-j\omega},\qquad K=\lim_{s\to-\alpha-j\omega}\big[(s+\alpha+j\omega)Y(s)\big]
$$

Inverting gives $Ke^{-(\alpha+j\omega)t}+K^*e^{-(\alpha-j\omega)t}$. The two terms are conjugates, and $z+z^*=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,z$, so with $K=a+jb$:

$$
Ke^{-(\alpha+j\omega)t}+K^*e^{-(\alpha-j\omega)t}=2e^{-\alpha t}\,\mathrm{Re}\{(a+jb)(\cos\omega t-j\sin\omega t)\}=2e^{-\alpha t}\big(a\cos\omega t+b\sin\omega t\big)
$$

<KeyFormula title="Conjugate-pair rule">

$$
\frac{K}{s+\alpha+j\omega}+\frac{K^*}{s+\alpha-j\omega}\ \xrightarrow{\ \Li\ }\ 2e^{-\alpha t}\big(a\cos\omega t+b\sin\omega t\big),\qquad K=a+jb
$$

</KeyFormula>

You only ever compute **one** complex coefficient; its conjugate partner comes for free. The professor does the same thing directly with Euler's formula $\sin x=\dfrac{e^{jx}-e^{-jx}}{2j}$ (p. 6) — both routes give the same result.

## Unit impulse response

<Term en="Unit impulse response" tr="birim impulse tepkesi" />: $r(t)=\delta(t)$, $R(s)=1$, so $Y(s)=T(s)$. That is why the professor writes the transfer function directly as $Y(s)$. <Src p="3" />

### Impulse: overdamped

With real distinct roots <Src p="3" />:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+p_1)(s+p_2)}=\frac{K_1}{s+p_1}+\frac{K_2}{s+p_2}
$$

The notes leave $K_1,K_2$ unevaluated; with the cover-up rule:

$$
K_1=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s+p_2}\right|_{s=-p_1}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2-p_1},\qquad K_2=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s+p_1}\right|_{s=-p_2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_1-p_2}=-\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2-p_1}
$$

Inverse Laplace (<Src p="4" />, the notes write lower-case $k_1,k_2$ — same coefficients):

$$
y(t)=k_1e^{-p_1t}+k_2e^{-p_2t}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2-p_1}\left(e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t}\right)=\frac{\omega_n}{2\sqrt{\xi^2-1}}\left(e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t}\right)
$$

(using $p_2-p_1=2\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}$). Since $p_1<p_2$, $e^{-p_1t}>e^{-p_2t}$ for $t>0$: the response is **positive**, starts at 0, rises to a single hump and decays — no oscillation, no zero crossing. The slow exponential $e^{-p_1t}$ governs the long tail.

### Impulse: critically damped

<Src p="5" /> With the repeated root $s_{1,2}=-\omega_n$:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+\omega_n)^2}\quad\xrightarrow{\ \Li\{1/(s+p)^2\}=te^{-pt}\ }\quad y(t)=\omega_n^2\,t\,e^{-\omega_nt}
$$

No partial fractions are even needed: it is already a single table entry. The hump peaks where $\frac{d}{dt}(te^{-\omega_nt})=e^{-\omega_nt}(1-\omega_nt)=0$, i.e. at $t=1/\omega_n$, with height $\omega_n/e$.

<Note title="Correction to the notes (p. 5)">

The notes write "$s_{1,2}=\omega_n$" for the repeated root. A minus sign is missing: the root is $s_{1,2}=-\omega_n$ (as correctly written on pp. 2, 8 and 12). The formula $Y(s)=\omega_n^2/(s+\omega_n)^2$ on the same page is right, and $s+\omega_n=0$ gives $s=-\omega_n$.

</Note>

### Impulse: underdamped

<Src p="5" /> Complex roots $s_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm j\omega$; with $p_1=\alpha+j\omega$, $p_2=\alpha-j\omega$:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+p_1)(s+p_2)}=\frac{K}{s+p_1}+\frac{K^*}{s+p_2}
$$

Cover-up at $s=-p_1=-\alpha-j\omega$ (the other factor $s+p_2=s+\alpha-j\omega$ becomes $-2j\omega$ there):

$$
K=\lim_{s\to-\alpha-j\omega}\big[(s+\alpha+j\omega)Y(s)\big]=\frac{\omega_n^2}{-\alpha-j\omega+\alpha-j\omega}=\frac{-\omega_n^2}{2j\omega}
$$

$$
K^*=\lim_{s\to-\alpha+j\omega}\big[(s+\alpha-j\omega)Y(s)\big]=\frac{\omega_n^2}{-\alpha+j\omega+\alpha+j\omega}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{2j\omega}
$$

Inverse Laplace, then factor out $e^{-\alpha t}$ and use Euler's identity $\sin x=\frac{e^{jx}-e^{-jx}}{2j}$ <Src p="6" />:

$$
y(t)=\frac{-\omega_n^2}{2j\omega}e^{-(\alpha+j\omega)t}+\frac{\omega_n^2}{2j\omega}e^{-(\alpha-j\omega)t}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\underbrace{\left(\frac{e^{j\omega t}-e^{-j\omega t}}{2j}\right)}_{\sin\omega t}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t
$$

Finally $\omega_n^2/\omega=\omega_n^2/(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2})=\omega_n/\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$:

<KeyFormula title="Underdamped unit impulse response (p. 6)">

$$
y(t)=\frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\,e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t
$$

</KeyFormula>

Check with the conjugate-pair rule: $K=\frac{-\omega_n^2}{2j\omega}=j\frac{\omega_n^2}{2\omega}$, so $a=0$, $b=\frac{\omega_n^2}{2\omega}$ and $2e^{-\alpha t}(b\sin\omega t)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t$ ✓.

**Physical meaning:** a sine at the damped frequency $\omega$ inside an exponentially shrinking envelope $\pm\frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}$. The zero crossings are at $\omega t=k\pi$, i.e. every $\pi/\omega$ seconds.

### Impulse response family

<Src p="4" /> The notes show the impulse response for $\xi=0.25$, $1$ and $2$ (no $\omega_n$ given; here $\omega_n=1$, so the time axis is in units of $1/\omega_n$ and for another $\omega_n$ you rescale $t$ by $1/\omega_n$ and $y$ by $\omega_n$). Exact functions plotted:

- $\xi=0.25$: $y=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.9375}}e^{-0.25t}\sin(0.968t)$ — tall first peak, then a negative lobe, a smaller positive one…: a decaying oscillation about 0.
- $\xi=1$: $y=te^{-t}$ — one hump, peak $1/e=0.368$ at $t=1$, no zero crossing.
- $\xi=2$: $y=\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}\left(e^{-0.268t}-e^{-3.732t}\right)$ — the lowest, flattest hump and the slowest tail.

"As seen, increasing the damping factor reduces the system oscillation" (<Term en="oscillation" tr="salınım" />). <Src p="4" />

**How to sketch by hand:** every curve starts at $y(0)=0$ with initial slope $y'(0^+)=\omega_n^2$ (the same for all $\xi$ — the impulse "kicks" the velocity). Underdamped: draw the envelope $\pm\frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}$, cross zero every $\pi/\omega$. Critical: one hump at $t=1/\omega_n$, height $\omega_n/e$. Overdamped: lower, wider hump with a long tail. All tend to 0.

### Steady-state error for the impulse

The steady-state error is the final value of $e(t)=r(t)-y(t)$; by the final value theorem <Src p="6" />:

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\big[R(s)-Y(s)\big]=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(1-\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}\right)=0\cdot(1-1)=0
$$

The impulse is over at $t=0^+$ and the output dies out, so the error is zero.

<Note title="Correction to the notes (pp. 6, 7, 8, 10, 14)">

In several $e_{ss}$ and $Y(s)$ expressions the notes write the denominator as $s^2+2\xi\omega_n+\omega_n^2$ — the $s$ in the middle term is missing. The correct denominator is always $s^2+2\xi\omega_n\,s+\omega_n^2$. The slip does not change any limit computed at $s=0$ (the middle term vanishes there anyway), so all of the professor's results stand. On p. 8 the formula for $K_1$ also still shows $\xi$ although $\xi=1$ in that section — again harmless at $s=0$.

</Note>

## Unit step response

<Term en="Unit step response" tr="birim basamak tepkesi" />: $r(t)=1$ for $t\ge0$, $R(s)=\frac1s$ <Src p="7" />:

$$
Y(s)=\frac1s\cdot\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}
$$

The extra $\frac1s$ adds one more partial fraction, $\frac{K_1}{s}$, which becomes the constant final value.

### Step: overdamped

<Src p="7" /> All roots real and distinct ($0,\,-p_1,\,-p_2$):

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+p_1)(s+p_2)}=\frac{K_1}{s}+\frac{K_2}{s+p_1}+\frac{K_3}{s+p_2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t)=K_1+K_2e^{-p_1t}+K_3e^{-p_2t}
$$

The notes stop here. The coefficients by the cover-up rule, simplified with $p_1p_2=\omega_n^2$:

$$
K_1=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+p_1)(s+p_2)}\right|_{s=0}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_1p_2}=1
$$

$$
K_2=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+p_2)}\right|_{s=-p_1}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{-p_1(p_2-p_1)}=-\frac{p_2}{p_2-p_1},\qquad K_3=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+p_1)}\right|_{s=-p_2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{-p_2(p_1-p_2)}=\frac{p_1}{p_2-p_1}
$$

<KeyFormula title="Overdamped unit step response">

$$
y(t)=1-\frac{p_2\,e^{-p_1t}-p_1\,e^{-p_2t}}{p_2-p_1},\qquad p_2-p_1=2\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Checks.** $y(0)=1-\frac{p_2-p_1}{p_2-p_1}=0$ ✓. $y(\infty)=1$ ✓. Differentiate: $\dot y=\frac{p_1p_2}{p_2-p_1}(e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t})=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2-p_1}(e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t})$ — exactly the overdamped impulse response, as it must be (the step is the integral of the impulse). Since $\dot y>0$ for all $t>0$, the overdamped step rises **monotonically** and never overshoots.

### Step: critically damped

<Src p="8" /> Repeated root $s_{1,2}=-\omega_n$; the professor sets $p=\omega_n$:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\cdot\frac1s=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+\omega_n)^2}\cdot\frac1s=\frac{K_1}{s}+\frac{K_2}{s+p}+\frac{K_3}{(s+p)^2}
$$

Coefficients (the notes compute them in the order $K_1$, $K_3$, $K_2$ — the derivative coefficient needs the same bracket as $K_3$):

$$
K_1=\lim_{s\to0}\big[sY(s)\big]=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\omega_n s+\omega_n^2}\right|_{s=0}=1
$$

$$
K_3=\lim_{s\to-p}\big[(s+\omega_n)^2Y(s)\big]=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s}\right|_{s=-\omega_n}=-\omega_n
$$

$$
K_2=\lim_{s\to-p}\frac{d}{ds}\big[(s+\omega_n)^2Y(s)\big]=\lim_{s\to-p}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\omega_n^2}{s}\right)=\left.\frac{-\omega_n^2}{s^2}\right|_{s=-\omega_n}=-1
$$

Inverse Laplace ($\frac1s\to1$, $\frac{1}{s+p}\to e^{-pt}$, $\frac{1}{(s+p)^2}\to te^{-pt}$):

<KeyFormula title="Critically damped unit step response (p. 8)">

$$
y(t)=1-e^{-\omega_nt}-\omega_nt\,e^{-\omega_nt}
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Checks.** $y(0)=1-1-0=0$ ✓, $y(\infty)=1$ ✓, $\dot y=\omega_ne^{-\omega_nt}-\omega_ne^{-\omega_nt}+\omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}=\omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}$ = the critical impulse response ✓. Recombining: $\frac1s-\frac{1}{s+\omega_n}-\frac{\omega_n}{(s+\omega_n)^2}=\frac{(s+\omega_n)^2-s(s+\omega_n)-\omega_ns}{s(s+\omega_n)^2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s(s+\omega_n)^2}$ ✓.

### Step: underdamped

<Src p="9" /> Complex roots $s_{1,2}=-\alpha\pm j\omega$, $p_1=\alpha+j\omega$, $p_2=\alpha-j\omega$:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{K_1}{s}+\frac{K_2}{s+p_1}+\frac{K_2^*}{s+p_2}
$$

The notes say "applying the coefficient-finding methods given earlier" and jump to the result. Here are the missing steps.

**$K_1$:** $K_1=\left.\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\right|_{s=0}=1$.

**$K_2$:** cover up $(s+p_1)$ and substitute $s=-\alpha-j\omega$. The remaining factors are $s$ and $s+p_2=s+\alpha-j\omega$, which equals $-2j\omega$ at that point:

$$
K_2=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(-\alpha-j\omega)(-2j\omega)}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{2j\alpha\omega+2j^2\omega^2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{-2\omega(\omega-j\alpha)}
$$

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $(\omega+j\alpha)$ and use $\omega^2+\alpha^2=\omega_n^2$:

$$
K_2=-\frac{\omega_n^2(\omega+j\alpha)}{2\omega(\omega^2+\alpha^2)}=-\frac{\omega+j\alpha}{2\omega}=-\frac12-j\frac{\alpha}{2\omega}
$$

**Invert** with the conjugate-pair rule ($a=-\frac12$, $b=-\frac{\alpha}{2\omega}$):

$$
y(t)=1+2e^{-\alpha t}\left(-\frac12\cos\omega t-\frac{\alpha}{2\omega}\sin\omega t\right)=1-e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cos\omega t+\frac{\alpha}{\omega}\sin\omega t\right)
$$

and $\dfrac{\alpha}{\omega}=\dfrac{\xi\omega_n}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=\dfrac{\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$:

<KeyFormula title="Underdamped unit step response (p. 9)">

$$
y(t)=1-e^{-\alpha t}\left[\cos\omega t+\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\sin\omega t\right]
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Checks.** $y(0)=1-[1+0]=0$ ✓; $y(\infty)=1$ ✓.

### Amplitude-phase form of the step response

The professor then "combines the sinusoidal terms" (<Term en="sinusoidal terms" tr="sinüzoidal terimler" />) <Src p="9" />. The identity behind it:

$$
A\cos x+B\sin x=R\sin(x+\varphi),\qquad R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2},\quad \sin\varphi=\frac AR,\quad\cos\varphi=\frac BR
$$

(Proof: expand $R\sin(x+\varphi)=R\sin\varphi\cos x+R\cos\varphi\sin x$ and compare coefficients.) Here $A=1$, $B=\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$:

$$
R=\sqrt{1+\frac{\xi^2}{1-\xi^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-\xi^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}},\qquad \sin\varphi=\frac{1}{R}=\sqrt{1-\xi^2},\qquad\cos\varphi=\frac BR=\xi
$$

So $\varphi$ is exactly the pole angle $\theta$ ($\cos\theta=\xi$) and $\tan\theta=\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}$:

<KeyFormula title="Step response, amplitude-phase form (p. 9)">

$$
y(t)=1-\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\sin(\omega t+\theta),\qquad \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}
$$

</KeyFormula>

At $t=0$: $\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=1$, so $y(0)=0$ ✓. For $0<\xi<1$, $\theta$ lies between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, so the calculator's $\tan^{-1}$ gives the right angle directly.

<Mistake title="Degrees vs radians">

In $\sin(\omega t+\theta)$, $\omega t$ is in **radians**. Either use $\theta$ in radians ($\theta[\text{rad}]=\theta[^\circ]\cdot\pi/180$) or convert $\omega t$ to degrees. Mixing them (e.g. $\sin(4.58\cdot0.5+66.42)$ with the calculator in radian mode) gives garbage.

</Mistake>

**What the formula says physically:** the output oscillates around the final value 1 at the damped frequency $\omega$, inside the envelope $1\pm\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$. A smaller $\xi$ gives a slower decay $\alpha=\xi\omega_n$ and a larger overshoot. Because $\dot y$ equals the impulse response $\frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t$, the step's peaks and valleys are where $\sin\omega t=0$: $t=\pi/\omega,\ 2\pi/\omega,\dots$ At the first peak $t=\pi/\omega$, $\cos\omega t=-1$, $\sin\omega t=0$, so $y_{\max}=1+e^{-\alpha\pi/\omega}$.

### Why every step response starts flat

<InitialSlopeZoom />

<Note title="Correction to the notes' sketch (p. 10)">

In the hand sketch the $\xi=1$ and $\xi=2$ curves leave the origin with a visible non-zero slope. The true step response of a second-order system has **zero initial slope for every $\xi$**. Two ways to see it:

1. **Derivative = impulse response.** $\dot y_{\text{step}}(t)=y_{\text{impulse}}(t)$, and all three impulse responses start at $0$ ($\omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}$, $\sin\omega t$, $e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t}$ all vanish at $t=0$). So $\dot y(0^+)=0$.
2. **Initial value theorem** applied to $\dot y$: $\L\{\dot y\}=sY(s)-y(0)=sY(s)=T(s)$, hence

$$
\dot y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\cdot\big[sY(s)\big]=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{s\,\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}=0
$$

Applying the theorem once more gives $\ddot y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\big[s^2Y(s)\big]=\omega_n^2$, so near $t=0$ every step response looks like $\frac{\omega_n^2t^2}{2}$ (dashed curve above). Physically: a step force cannot instantly give a mass a velocity, only an acceleration. The professor's ordering of the curves (0.25 fastest, then 1, then 2) is correct; only the starting slope of the sketch is off.

</Note>

### Steady-state error for the step

<Src p="10" />

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\big[R(s)-Y(s)\big]=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac1s-\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\cdot\frac1s\right)=\lim_{s\to0}\left(1-\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\right)=1-1=0
$$

The standard second-order system follows a step with **zero** steady-state error in all three damping cases — because its DC gain is 1 (its loop contains an integrator). If the closed loop has DC gain $K\ne1$, then $e_{ss}=1-K$ (Example 2 gives 0.2).

## Unit ramp response

<Term en="Unit ramp response" tr="birim rampa tepkesi" />: $r(t)=t$, $R(s)=\frac{1}{s^2}$ <Src p="11" />:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{1}{s^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}
$$

Now $s=0$ is a **double** root, so there are two terms $\frac{K_1}{s^2}+\frac{K_2}{s}$, and $K_2$ needs the derivative rule. These two terms are the same in all three cases:

$$
K_1=\lim_{s\to0}\big[s^2Y(s)\big]=\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega_n^2}=1,\qquad K_2=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{d}{ds}\left[\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\right]=\left.\frac{-\omega_n^2(2s+2\xi\omega_n)}{(s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)^2}\right|_{s=0}=\frac{-2\xi\omega_n^3}{\omega_n^4}=-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}
$$

So **every** ramp response contains $t-\dfrac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$: after the transient, the output is a line of slope 1 that lags the input by $\dfrac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$.

### Ramp: overdamped

<Src p="11" />

$$
Y(s)=\frac{K_1}{s^2}+\frac{K_2}{s}+\frac{K_3}{s+p_1}+\frac{K_4}{s+p_2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t)=K_1t+K_2+K_3e^{-p_1t}+K_4e^{-p_2t}
$$

The notes leave the coefficients unevaluated. $K_1=1$, $K_2=-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ (above), and by cover-up with $p_1p_2=\omega_n^2$:

$$
K_3=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2(s+p_2)}\right|_{s=-p_1}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_1^2(p_2-p_1)}=\frac{p_2}{p_1(p_2-p_1)},\qquad K_4=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2(s+p_1)}\right|_{s=-p_2}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2^2(p_1-p_2)}=-\frac{p_1}{p_2(p_2-p_1)}
$$

<KeyFormula title="Overdamped unit ramp response">

$$
y(t)=t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac{1}{p_2-p_1}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}e^{-p_1t}-\frac{p_1}{p_2}e^{-p_2t}\right)
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Check** $y(0)=0$: $\frac{1}{p_2-p_1}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}-\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)=\frac{p_2^2-p_1^2}{p_1p_2(p_2-p_1)}=\frac{p_1+p_2}{p_1p_2}=\frac{2\xi\omega_n}{\omega_n^2}=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$, which cancels $-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ ✓.

### Ramp: critically damped

<Src p="12" /> With $p=\omega_n$, repeated roots $s_{1,2}=-\omega_n$:

$$
Y(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{(s+\omega_n)^2}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2}=\frac{K_1}{s^2}+\frac{K_2}{s}+\frac{K_3}{(s+p)^2}+\frac{K_4}{s+p}
$$

Two double roots, so two derivative coefficients:

$$
K_1=\lim_{s\to0}\big[s^2Y(s)\big]=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\right|_{s=0}=1
$$

$$
K_2=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\right)=\left.\frac{-\omega_n^2(2s+2\omega_n)}{(s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)^2}\right|_{s=0}=\frac{-2\omega_n^3}{\omega_n^4}=-\frac{2}{\omega_n}
$$

$$
K_3=\lim_{s\to-\omega_n}\big[(s+\omega_n)^2Y(s)\big]=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2}\right|_{s=-\omega_n}=1
$$

$$
K_4=\lim_{s\to-\omega_n}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2}\right)=\left.\frac{-2s\,\omega_n^2}{s^4}\right|_{s=-\omega_n}=\left.\frac{-2\omega_n^2}{s^3}\right|_{s=-\omega_n}=\frac{2}{\omega_n}
$$

<Src p="13" /> (The notes write the derivative in quotient-rule form $-2s\omega_n^2/s^4$; it simplifies to $-2\omega_n^2/s^3$.)

<KeyFormula title="Critically damped unit ramp response (p. 13)">

$$
y(t)=t-\frac{2}{\omega_n}+t\,e^{-\omega_nt}+\frac{2}{\omega_n}e^{-\omega_nt}
$$

</KeyFormula>

**Checks.** $y(0)=-\frac2{\omega_n}+\frac2{\omega_n}=0$ ✓. $\dot y=1-\omega_nte^{-\omega_nt}+e^{-\omega_nt}-2e^{-\omega_nt}=1-e^{-\omega_nt}-\omega_nte^{-\omega_nt}$ — the critical step response ✓ (the ramp is the integral of the step). With $\xi=1$, $\frac{2}{\omega_n}=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ ✓.

<Note title="Correction to the notes (p. 12)">

In $K_2$ the notes write the squared denominator as $(s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n)^2$; the last term must be $\omega_n^2$: $(s^2+2\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)^2$. With the correct denominator the value at $s=0$ is $\omega_n^4$ and $K_2=-2\omega_n^3/\omega_n^4=-2/\omega_n$, exactly the professor's result.

</Note>

### Ramp: underdamped

<Src p="13" />

$$
Y(s)=\frac{K_1}{s^2}+\frac{K_2}{s}+\frac{K_3}{s+p_1}+\frac{K_3^*}{s+p_2},\qquad p_1=\alpha+j\omega,\ p_2=\alpha-j\omega
$$

$K_1=1$, $K_2=-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ as for every case. For $K_3$ the polar form of the root is the cleanest route. From the [geometry of the roots](#/m/07/geometry-of-the-complex-roots), $-\alpha-j\omega=-\omega_n(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=-\omega_ne^{j\theta}$, so $s^2=\omega_n^2e^{j2\theta}$ at that root, and $s+p_2=-2j\omega$ there:

$$
K_3=\left.\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2(s+\alpha-j\omega)}\right|_{s=-\alpha-j\omega}=\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega_n^2e^{j2\theta}\,(-2j\omega)}=\frac{e^{-j2\theta}}{-2j\omega}=\frac{j\,e^{-j2\theta}}{2\omega}=\frac{\sin2\theta+j\cos2\theta}{2\omega}
$$

(using $\frac{1}{-j}=j$ and $je^{-j2\theta}=j\cos2\theta+\sin2\theta$). Conjugate-pair rule with $a=\frac{\sin2\theta}{2\omega}$, $b=\frac{\cos2\theta}{2\omega}$:

$$
2e^{-\alpha t}\left(\frac{\sin2\theta}{2\omega}\cos\omega t+\frac{\cos2\theta}{2\omega}\sin\omega t\right)=\frac1\omega e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)
$$

(sine addition formula $\sin(x+y)=\sin x\cos y+\cos x\sin y$).

<KeyFormula title="Underdamped unit ramp response (corrected phase)">

$$
y(t)=t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac{1}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta),\qquad \theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}
$$

</KeyFormula>

<Src p="14" /> The notes define $\theta$ on p. 14 exactly as in the step response.

<Note title="Correction to the notes (pp. 13 and 17): the phase is 2θ, not θ">

The notes give $y(t)=t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac1\omega e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+\theta)$. With $\theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}$ (as defined on p. 14) the phase must be $2\theta$. The quickest test is $y(0)=0$ (the output cannot jump):

- with $2\theta$: $\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta=2\xi\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$, so $y(0)=-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac{2\xi\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=0$ ✓;
- with $\theta$: $y(0)=-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=\frac{1-2\xi}{\omega_n}$, which is non-zero unless $\xi=0.5$ ✗.

A numerical solution of the ODE confirms the $2\theta$ version (see [Example 2](#/m/07/ramp-response-of-example-2), where the phase becomes $132.84^\circ$ instead of $66.42^\circ$). The amplitude $\frac{1}{\omega}$ and the offset $-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ in the notes are correct.

</Note>

### Why the phase is 2 theta

There is a pattern across the three inputs. Each extra $\frac1s$ in $R(s)$ multiplies the complex coefficient by $\frac1s$ evaluated at the pole, $\frac{1}{-\omega_ne^{j\theta}}$ — i.e. it **rotates the phase by $\theta$** (and divides the size by $\omega_n$):

| Input | $R(s)$ | Oscillating part of $y(t)$ |
|---|---|---|
| impulse | $1$ | $\dfrac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t)$ — phase $0$ |
| step | $\dfrac1s$ | $-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+\theta)$ — phase $\theta$ |
| ramp | $\dfrac{1}{s^2}$ | $+\dfrac{1}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)$ — phase $2\theta$ |

The same thing seen in the time domain: the ramp response is the integral of the step response. Differentiating $e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+\varphi)$ gives $e^{-\alpha t}(\omega\cos(\cdot)-\alpha\sin(\cdot))=\omega_ne^{-\alpha t}\big(\sin\theta\cos(\cdot)-\cos\theta\sin(\cdot)\big)=-\omega_ne^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+\varphi-\theta)$. So

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left[-\frac{1}{\omega_n}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)\right]=e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+\theta)
$$

and integrating the step response from 0 to $t$:

$$
\int_0^t\left[1-\frac{e^{-\alpha\tau}\sin(\omega\tau+\theta)}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\right]d\tau=t-\frac{\sin2\theta-e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac1\omega e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)
$$

(using $\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}=\omega$ and $\frac{\sin2\theta}{\omega}=\frac{2\xi\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$). Each integration adds $\theta$ to the phase: impulse $0$ → step $\theta$ → ramp $2\theta$.

### Steady-state error for the ramp

<Src p="14" />

$$
e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\left(\frac{1}{s^2}-\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2}\right)=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns}{s(s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s+2\xi\omega_n}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}
$$

<KeyFormula title="Ramp steady-state error (p. 14)">

$$
e_{ss,\text{ramp}}=\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}
$$

</KeyFormula>

This is the constant gap between $r(t)=t$ and the asymptote $t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ — it agrees with the time-domain result. More damping → more lag; a faster system (larger $\omega_n$) → less lag. That is why the $\xi=2$ line in the figure lies furthest below $r(t)$.

impulse $e_{ss}=0$ · step $e_{ss}=0$ · ramp $e_{ss}=2\xi/\omega_n$. These hold for $T(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$, i.e. for the loop of p. 1 with its integrator. For any other loop, compute $\lim_{s\to0}s[R-Y]$ again (Example 2 gives 0, 0.2, ∞).

<Example2BD />

<Example title="Example 2: impulse, step and ramp responses" src="15–17">

**Given:** unity negative feedback around $G(s)=\dfrac{20}{s^2+4s+5}$. **Asked:** the <Term en="unit impulse, unit step and unit ramp responses" tr="birim impulse, birim basamak ve birim rampa cevapları" /> (and, as the notes go on to do, the steady-state error for each).

**Method:** closed loop → write $T(s)$ as (DC gain) × (standard form) → read $\omega_n,\xi$ → use the underdamped formulas multiplied by the DC gain → $e_{ss}$ from the final value theorem.

<Step title="Closed loop in standard form">

$$
T(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{\dfrac{20}{s^2+4s+5}}{1+\dfrac{20}{s^2+4s+5}}=\frac{20}{s^2+4s+25}=\frac{0.8\cdot(5)^2}{s^2+2\cdot0.4\cdot5\,s+(5)^2}

</Step>

<Step title="Roots and parameters">

$$
s_{1,2}=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{16-100}}{2}=-2\pm j\frac{\sqrt{84}}{2}=-2\pm j4.58

so $\alpha=\xi\omega_n=2$, $\omega=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}=5\sqrt{0.84}=4.583$ rad/s, $\theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{0.84}}{0.4}=66.42^\circ=1.159$ rad.

</Step>

<Step title="Unit impulse response">

## Edge cases and limits

### Undamped limit

With $\xi=0$: $\alpha=0$, $\omega=\omega_n$, $\theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{0}=90^\circ$, roots $\pm j\omega_n$ on the imaginary axis. The underdamped formulas (valid as a limit) give

$$
\text{impulse: }y=\omega_n\sin\omega_nt,\qquad\text{step: }y=1-\sin(\omega_nt+90^\circ)=1-\cos\omega_nt,\qquad\text{ramp: }y=t-0+\frac{1}{\omega_n}\sin(\omega_nt+180^\circ)=t-\frac{\sin\omega_nt}{\omega_n}
$$

The oscillation never decays (an ideal LC circuit, a frictionless spring): the step response swings between 0 and 2 forever, and "steady state" does not exist — the final value theorem does not apply because $Y(s)$ has poles on the imaginary axis. Here $e_{ss}=2\xi/\omega_n=0$ would be wrong to quote: the ramp error $\frac{\sin\omega_nt}{\omega_n}$ oscillates forever.

### Approaching critical damping

As $\xi\to1^-$: $\omega\to0$, $\alpha\to\omega_n$. Since $\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\to t$ for $\omega\to0$,

$$
\frac{\omega_n^2}{\omega}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t\ \longrightarrow\ \omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}
$$

— the underdamped impulse response turns smoothly into the critical one. Likewise $\cos\omega t\to1$ and $\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\sin\omega t=\xi\omega_n\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\to\omega_nt$, so the step tends to $1-e^{-\omega_nt}(1+\omega_nt)$ ✓. From above ($\xi\to1^+$), $p_1,p_2\to\omega_n$ and $\frac{e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t}}{p_2-p_1}\to te^{-\omega_nt}$ (a derivative quotient). The formulas change shape at $\xi=1$ only because the partial-fraction structure changes (two different roots → one repeated root); the physical response itself changes continuously. That is why the critical case needs the derivative formula.

<LimitPlots />

### Non-unity DC gain and non-standard loops

If $T(s)=\dfrac{b}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$ with $b\ne\omega_n^2$, write $T=K\cdot\dfrac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$ with $K=b/\omega_n^2$ and multiply **every** response by $K$ (Examples 1 and 2). The steady-state errors change: step $e_{ss}=1-K$, ramp $e_{ss}=\infty$ unless $K=1$.

## Summary of results

Standard form $T(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$; $p_{1,2}=\xi\omega_n\mp\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}$; $\alpha=\xi\omega_n$; $\omega=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$; $\theta=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}{\xi}$.

| | Overdamped $\xi>1$ | Critical $\xi=1$ | Underdamped $0<\xi<1$ | $e_{ss}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Impulse | $\frac{\omega_n^2}{p_2-p_1}(e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t})$ | $\omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}$ | $\frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t$ | $0$ |
| Step | $1-\frac{p_2e^{-p_1t}-p_1e^{-p_2t}}{p_2-p_1}$ | $1-e^{-\omega_nt}-\omega_nte^{-\omega_nt}$ | $1-\frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}\sin(\omega t+\theta)$ | $0$ |
| Ramp | $t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac{1}{p_2-p_1}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}e^{-p_1t}-\frac{p_1}{p_2}e^{-p_2t}\right)$ | $t-\frac2{\omega_n}+te^{-\omega_nt}+\frac2{\omega_n}e^{-\omega_nt}$ | $t-\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}+\frac1\omega e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t+2\theta)$ | $\frac{2\xi}{\omega_n}$ |

## How to solve exam problems

<Procedure title="Second-order time response">

1. **Get the closed loop.** If a loop is drawn, $T=\frac{G}{1+GH}$ (unity: $\frac{G}{1+G}$). Clear fractions so the denominator is $s^2+as+b$ (divide by the leading coefficient if needed).
2. **Read the parameters.** $\omega_n=\sqrt b$, $\xi=\frac{a}{2\omega_n}$, DC gain $K=\frac{\text{numerator}}{\omega_n^2}$.
3. **Classify.** $\xi>1$ aşırı sönümlü, $\xi=1$ kritik sönümlü, $\xi<1$ düşük sönümlü. Compute the roots ($p_1,p_2$ or $\alpha,\omega,\theta$).
4. **Form $Y(s)=T(s)R(s)$** with $R=1$, $\frac1s$ or $\frac1{s^2}$.
5. **Partial fractions:** cover-up for simple roots, derivative rule for repeated roots (and for $\frac1{s^2}$), one complex coefficient $K$ for the conjugate pair.
6. **Invert** and simplify; for complex roots use the conjugate-pair rule, then (if asked) the amplitude-phase form — step phase $\theta$, ramp phase $2\theta$.
7. **Multiply by $K$** if the DC gain is not 1.
8. **Verify:** $y(0)=0$ for step and ramp; $\dot y(0)=0$ for the step; $y(\infty)$ from the final value theorem; derivative of step = impulse.
9. **Steady-state error:** $e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s[R(s)-Y(s)]$ for the actual system.

</Procedure>

<Mistake title="Typical exam slips">

- Taking $\omega_n$, $\xi$ from the **open-loop** $G(s)$ instead of the closed-loop $T(s)$.
- Forgetting the DC gain factor (Example 2's 0.8).
- Writing $\theta$ instead of $2\theta$ in the underdamped ramp response (the notes do this too).
- Mixing degrees and radians inside $\sin(\omega t+\theta)$.
- Writing the critical root as $+\omega_n$ (sign slip, as on p. 5) — poles of a stable system have negative real parts.
- Forgetting the derivative term for a repeated root or for the double pole at $s=0$ of a ramp input.
- Quoting $e_{ss}=2\xi/\omega_n$ for a loop without an integrator.
- Sketching a step response that leaves the origin with a slope.

</Mistake>

<Tip title="Recognise the problem type quickly">

"Türünü ve parametrelerini bulunuz" → steps 1–3 only. "Birim impulse / basamak / rampa cevaplarını bulunuz" → steps 1–8 for each input. "Kalıcı durum hatası" → step 9. If the numbers are "nice" (e.g. roots $-3\pm j4$), computing $K$ numerically, as in the $\xi=0.6$ example, is often faster and less error-prone than plugging into the general formulas.

</Tip>

<Exercise title="Name the three damping cases" kind="concept" level={1}>

For $T(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$, give the Turkish and English names of the three cases, the condition on $\xi$, the type of roots, and what the step response looks like in each.

<Solution>

From $s_{1,2}=-\xi\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\xi^2-1}$:

- $\xi>1$: aşırı sönümlü (overdamped): real, distinct roots $-p_1,-p_2$; slow monotonic rise, no overshoot.
- $\xi=1$: kritik sönümlü (critically damped): real repeated root $-\omega_n$; fastest rise without overshoot.
- $0<\xi<1$: düşük sönümlü (underdamped): complex-conjugate roots $-\alpha\pm j\omega$; overshoot and decaying oscillation around 1.

Check: the sign of $\xi^2-1$ decides whether the square root is real, zero or imaginary.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Undamped responses" kind="edge" level={2}>

Let $\xi=0$, $\omega_n=2$. Using the underdamped formulas as a limit, find the impulse, step and ramp responses. Does the system have a steady-state error for the step?

<Solution>

$\alpha=0$, $\omega=2$, $\theta=90^\circ$. Impulse: $\frac{2}{1}\sin2t=2\sin2t$. Step: $1-\sin(2t+90^\circ)=1-\cos2t$. Ramp: $t-0+\frac12\sin(2t+180^\circ)=t-\frac12\sin2t$.

<Exercise title="Why the step response starts flat" kind="concept" level={2}>

Explain, in two different ways, why every unit step response of the standard second-order system has $\dot y(0^+)=0$, and why the sketch on p. 10 needs correcting.

<Solution>

(1) $\dot y_{\text{step}}=y_{\text{impulse}}$, and all impulse responses start at 0 ($\omega_n^2te^{-\omega_nt}$, $e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega t$, $e^{-p_1t}-e^{-p_2t}$ vanish at $t=0$). (2) IVT: $\dot y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\cdot sY(s)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{s\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\xi\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}=0$. Physically a force step changes acceleration, not velocity, instantly. The sketch draws the $\xi=1$ and $\xi=2$ curves leaving the origin with a slope; they should start tangent to the $t$-axis.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
